# Six-Hour Nights Can Feel Fine. That’s the Problem.

Getting accustomed to short nights can conceal a loss of attention. A long recovery night may help without finishing the job.

After two weeks with only four or six hours in bed each night, volunteers in a sleep laboratory were reporting only slight sleepiness. Their average test performance, meanwhile, was at its worst.

The finding makes *I’m used to it* a less reassuring verdict on a sleep schedule. In the 2003 experiment led by Hans Van Dongen, healthy adults received four, six or eight hours in bed nightly. In the shorter-night groups, sleepiness ratings rose early and then changed relatively little, while performance continued to deteriorate. [Original experiment](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/VanDongen2003CumulativeCost.pdf)

One test made that deterioration unusually easy to see. Watch a screen. When the signal appears, press a button. Keep doing it for ten minutes, with the next signal arriving at an unpredictable interval. The challenge is sustaining attention across the whole session; a few quick responses do not cancel out the slow ones. Researchers call this a psychomotor vigilance test. [Test method](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/bios601/Surveys/Belenky2003.pdf)

By the end of the experiment, attention lapses in the six-hour group were comparable to those measured after one night without sleep. That comparison applies to the test, not every ability a person uses in a day. But the mismatch matters: feeling accustomed to a routine was not evidence that attention had recovered. [Study results](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/VanDongen2003CumulativeCost.pdf)

There is an obvious next hope: make up for it with a long night. Does that restore what the short nights took away?

Sleep researcher Siobhan Banks and colleagues tested recovery in 2010. After five nights with only four hours in bed, adults were assigned different lengths of recovery opportunity. More time helped. Yet even after ten hours in bed, the group’s vigilance performance had not returned fully to its earlier level. This was a severe restriction schedule, not a prediction of what happens after every busy week. Improvement after one recovery night did not necessarily mean recovery was complete. [Recovery experiment](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article-pdf/33/8/1013/13665045/sleep-33-8-1013.pdf)

The two studies challenge different versions of the same reassurance. Becoming familiar with short nights does not establish that they are enough; doing better after extra sleep does not establish that the effects are gone. Neither experiment supplies a personal sleep requirement or a rule for repaying each missed hour.

For healthy adults aged 18–60, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society recommend regularly getting at least seven hours of sleep. Individual needs vary, and seven is not a guarantee of optimal performance for everyone. Crucially, the experiments allocated time **in bed**. That is the opportunity to sleep, not a guarantee of being asleep for the whole interval. [Consensus recommendation](https://www.aasm.org/resources/pdf/adultsleepdurationconsensus.pdf)

That distinction turns an abstract sleep goal into a practical question: how much room does the calendar leave? Count from when you can realistically settle down to when you must get up. A quiet, cool, dark bedroom can support sleep; it cannot create hours missing from the schedule. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute’s guidance begins with making enough time for sleep and keeping regular bed and wake times. [Healthy sleep guidance](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-deprivation/healthy-sleep-habits)

If poor sleep affects daily life, discuss it with a clinician. A one- to two-week record of sleep times, naps and daytime sleepiness can make the pattern clearer. Mention frequent loud snoring or waking gasping too. The record supports a conversation; it does not diagnose the cause. [Sleep evaluation](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/insomnia/diagnosis)

The point is not to distrust every good morning. It is to give *I’m used to it* its proper meaning: a description of how a routine feels, not proof that it is giving you enough rest.
