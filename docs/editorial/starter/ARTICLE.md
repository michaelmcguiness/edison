# Why longitude became a problem of time

Finding a ship’s east-west position required comparing two times. Keeping the distant reference available at sea was the hard part.

*Edison · 5-minute reading estimate · Draft, not published*

Imagine a ship whose navigator can announce that it is noon, yet still cannot give the vessel's east-west position. There is no contradiction. Observing the Sun can tell the navigator something about the time here. Finding longitude requires another piece of information: the time somewhere else, at that same moment. [1](https://www.nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/everyday-time-and-atomic-time-part-4)

Latitude and longitude ask different questions. Latitude locates a place north or south of the equator. A navigator could estimate it from the Sun's height at noon, together with tables accounting for the date. Longitude measures east or west from a chosen reference meridian, a line running north to south. The challenge was to connect an observation made aboard a moving ship with that distant reference. [1](https://www.nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/everyday-time-and-atomic-time-part-4) [2](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/eqtime)

## Two hours become thirty degrees

The connection is time. In mean solar time, 24 hours correspond to a full 360-degree circuit of longitude. Divide 360 by 24 and each hour of difference between two meridians represents 15 degrees. Four minutes represent one degree. This turns a geographical question into a comparison that can be expressed on a clock face. [1](https://www.nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/everyday-time-and-atomic-time-part-4)

Take an illustrative ship, using Greenwich as its reference. Suppose the navigator's observations, after the necessary corrections, put local mean solar time at 12 noon. A reliable timekeeper set to Greenwich mean time reads 2 p.m. Greenwich's day is two hours further along. The ship is therefore 30 degrees west of Greenwich: two hours multiplied by 15 degrees per hour. If the reference time were 10 a.m. instead, the ship would be 30 degrees east. These are invented readings to show the calculation, not a record of a voyage. [1](https://www.nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/everyday-time-and-atomic-time-part-4) [2](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/eqtime)

The same arithmetic exposes the cost of an unreliable timekeeper. An uncorrected error of one minute becomes a quarter of a degree of longitude. Imagine a clock that gains two seconds each day, with no allowance made for that gain. After 30 days, its accumulated one-minute error introduces that quarter-degree error into the calculation. Those numbers are a worked example, not a claim about any particular Harrison clock. An error in time has become an error in position. [1](https://www.nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/everyday-time-and-atomic-time-part-4)

## The Sun needs a correction too

There is a catch in the convenient phrase 'local time'. A sundial follows the actual Sun, giving apparent solar time. A uniform clock follows a steadier scale. The Sun's apparent daily motion varies through the year, so those two readings do not always agree. Mean solar time smooths out that variation; the difference between apparent and mean solar time is called the equation of time. [2](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/eqtime)

For the ship's calculation, the two times have to be expressed on a consistent basis. Otherwise, a predictable difference between Sun time and mean time can be mistaken for part of the east-west separation. That is why the numerical example begins after the observations have been corrected. The neat multiplication describes the principle, while the correction is part of making that principle useful. [2](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/eqtime)

## A reference that could survive the journey

Keeping reference time at sea was an engineering problem. John Harrison's early marine timekeeper H1 used interconnected balances to address the motion of a ship. He brought it to London in 1735; a sea trial followed in 1736. Later attempts did not proceed smoothly. H2 never went to trial after Harrison identified a flaw, and he spent 19 years working on H3. The short equation concealed a long struggle to make an instrument behave. [4](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/harrisons-clocks-longitude-problem)

The decisive change in form emerged from work on watches. Royal Museums Greenwich dates Harrison's H4 to 1759. Its collection record describes a watch made to Harrison's design by John Jefferys in 1753, whose performance helped point Harrison toward a smaller instrument rather than the much larger H3. H4 used a stable, high-frequency balance. In the museum's account, improving the watch became a route to the longitude solution. [3](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-79142)

A successful instrument still had to become something others could make. Following H4's trials, the Board of Longitude confirmed that it had met the strictest accuracy limits of the 1714 Act. But part of the remaining reward depended on demonstrating that other makers could produce similar timekeepers. The distinction matters: showing that one extraordinary device works is different from establishing a repeatable means of navigation. [4](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/harrisons-clocks-longitude-problem)

## The alternative clock was in the sky

A mechanical timekeeper was not the only way to obtain reference time. The lunar-distance method compared the observed angle between the Moon and a particular star with predicted angles in an almanac. The Moon's changing position could supply the missing time reference. Measuring that angle accurately from a moving ship was difficult, but the method pursued the same objective through astronomy rather than a carried clock. [1](https://www.nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/everyday-time-and-atomic-time-part-4)

Making such astronomy usable required work ashore. Nevil Maskelyne organized the Nautical Almanac, with data for 1767 ready for printing by the end of 1766. Precomputed information reduced the burden of lengthy calculations at sea. Behind the printed tables were people processing observations into usable data. The apparent simplicity of consulting a book depended on a continuing organization of mathematical work. [5](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/longitude-found-nevil-maskelyne-lunar-method)

Nor did a successful trial instantly replace existing navigation. Royal Museums Greenwich describes the need for further trials, training and better charts, while timekeepers remained rare and expensive. Navigators could combine older methods with the new ones. The practical solution therefore involved more than selecting the winning invention: instruments, observations, calculations and people had to work together. [5](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/longitude-found-nevil-maskelyne-lunar-method)

Seen this way, longitude offers a particular lesson about invention. The useful object is easiest to appreciate once its job is clear. A sea clock did not read a ship's position directly. It preserved a reference that made another observation interpretable. A lunar table could provide that reference by a different route. The achievement was to make a comparison possible, and then reliable enough to use far from the people and places that had established it. [1](https://www.nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/everyday-time-and-atomic-time-part-4) [3](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-79142) [5](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/longitude-found-nevil-maskelyne-lunar-method)

**Three takeaways**

- Longitude can be calculated from a difference in mean solar time: one hour corresponds to 15 degrees.
- A carried timekeeper and astronomical observations offered different ways to obtain the same missing reference.
- Reliable navigation required usable instruments, tables and trained people, beyond a successful invention.
